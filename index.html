1. At a glance

The potato (Solanum tuberosum) is a starchy crop plant in the nightshade family, Solanaceae. The edible part is a tuber: a swollen underground stem that stores nutrients, rather than a root. Originating in the Americas, potatoes were domesticated in the Andes of what is now southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia thousands of years ago. They later spread worldwide and became one of humanity's major staple foods.

Potatoes are grown in thousands of varieties, differing in size, shape, skin and flesh color, starch content, texture, flavor, and culinary suitability. They are eaten boiled, baked, roasted, mashed, and fried, and are also used to produce starch, animal feed, and some distilled spirits. The plant's green parts and light-exposed or damaged tubers can contain elevated levels of toxic glycoalkaloids, so proper handling and storage matter.

Key facts

  • Common name: Potato; also called spud in English.
  • Scientific name: Solanum tuberosum.
  • Family: Solanaceae, the nightshade family.
  • Plant type: Herbaceous perennial, commonly cultivated as an annual crop.
  • Edible portion: Underground stem tuber.
  • Domestication region: Andes, especially southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia.
  • Domestication period: Approximately 7,000–10,000 years ago.
  • Diversity: Thousands of cultivated varieties; the article reports about 5,000 worldwide.
  • Main food role: Starchy staple used in cuisines across the world.
  • Important safety issue: Green or heavily sprouted potatoes may contain increased glycoalkaloids.

2. Name and terminology

The English word potato came through Spanish patata, which is associated with the Taíno word batata, originally referring to the sweet potato. The word spud was first associated with a short, stout digging knife or dagger; its use for the potato developed later.

Useful terms:

  • Tuber: A swollen underground stem that stores energy and nutrients.
  • Eyes: Buds on the surface of a potato tuber from which new shoots can grow.
  • Stolons: Horizontal underground stems from which tubers develop.
  • Lenticels: Small pores on tuber surfaces that permit gas exchange.
  • Cultivar: A cultivated plant variety selected for particular characteristics.
  • Seed potato: A tuber grown and selected for planting; it is not necessarily a botanical seed.
  • New or baby potato: A young, immature tuber harvested while small and tender.
  • Hilling or earthing up: Mounding soil around the plant to cover developing tubers and protect them from light.

3. Botanical description and classification

Plant structure

Potato plants can grow to around one metre (three feet) tall. They have hairy stems and compound leaves with multiple leaflets. Their flowers can be white, pink, blue, or purple, often with yellow centers, and are pollinated by insects.

The plant forms tubers at the ends of underground stolons. These tubers store nutrients for the plant. Their eyes are arranged in a spiral pattern and contain buds capable of producing shoots. Lenticels on the surface allow gas exchange. Under suitable conditions, the plant can also produce small green fruits resembling cherry tomatoes; these are not the edible potato tubers and should not be confused with them.

Scientific classification

  • Kingdom: Plantae
  • Clade: Tracheophytes
  • Clade: Angiosperms
  • Clade: Eudicots
  • Clade: Asterids
  • Order: Solanales
  • Family: Solanaceae
  • Genus: Solanum
  • Species: Solanum tuberosum

The potato is related to tomatoes, peppers, and other members of Solanaceae. The family also contains plants that are toxic. The cultivated potato is only one member of a wider group of wild potato relatives, many of which are valuable sources of genetic diversity for crop improvement.

Diversity and genetics

The widely cultivated potato has complex genetics. The common cultivated form is generally tetraploid, with 48 chromosomes. Other cultivated potato species or groups can have different chromosome numbers and ploidy levels. Andean and Chilean potato populations have adaptations to different day-length conditions.

Potato genetic diversity is especially rich in South America. Wild relatives can provide traits useful for breeding, including resistance to diseases and pests. Modern breeding includes conventional selection and, in some cases, genetic engineering or gene-editing techniques. Research has explored traits such as disease resistance, reduced bruising, altered starch composition, and reduced formation of acrylamide during frying. Acceptance and regulation of genetically modified potatoes differ across countries.

4. Origin and history

Domestication in the Andes

Wild potato species occur across parts of the Americas, from the southern United States to southern Chile. Cultivated potatoes were domesticated by Indigenous peoples in the Andean region, particularly around southern Peru and northwestern Bolivia, near Lake Titicaca. Domestication is estimated to have begun around 7,000–10,000 years ago. Archaeological remains show that potatoes were being used in Peru thousands of years ago.

The Andes remain a major center of potato diversity, with many traditional varieties adapted to local climates, elevations, soils, and farming practices. Indigenous knowledge and selection have played a central role in preserving this diversity.

Spread around the world

Spanish colonizers introduced potatoes to Europe during the second half of the 16th century as part of the Columbian Exchange. Adoption was initially slow, but potatoes became an important crop and food source in Europe after the mid-18th century. They contributed to population growth and urbanization because they could produce substantial food yields.

From Europe, potatoes spread through maritime travel, trade, colonial networks, and migration to many other regions. Modern potatoes grown in North America largely arrived through European settlement.

The Great Irish Famine

Potatoes became central to the diets of many poor rural communities, including in Ireland. In 1845, late blight—caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans—spread rapidly and destroyed potato crops in Ireland and parts of the Scottish Highlands. The resulting crop failures were a major cause of the Great Irish Famine. The disaster demonstrated the risks of relying heavily on a narrow range of genetically similar crops.

Research and recognition

The International Potato Center, based in Lima, Peru, conserves potato germplasm, including traditional landraces. Sequencing of the potato genome has helped researchers study its biology and improve breeding. The United Nations' International Day of Potato is observed on 30 May, beginning in 2024, to recognize the crop's importance to food security, livelihoods, and culture.

5. Potato varieties and choosing potatoes

The article reports roughly 5,000 varieties worldwide, with a large concentration in the Andes. Varieties differ in skin and flesh color, shape, size, maturity, disease resistance, starch content, and storage behavior.

Culinary texture

A practical distinction is between floury/mealy potatoes and waxy potatoes:

  • Floury or mealy potatoes generally have more starch and tend to break down more readily when cooked. They are often suitable for baking, roasting, and fluffy mashed potatoes.
  • Waxy potatoes tend to hold their shape after boiling and are useful for potato salads, soups, and dishes where intact pieces are desired.
  • All-purpose potatoes fall between these broad categories and can work in several preparations.
  • Chipping potatoes are selected for qualities suitable for making chips or crisps, such as appropriate shape, firmness, and processing characteristics.
  • New potatoes are harvested immature. They are usually small, tender, and lower in starch, with loose skins.

These categories are guides rather than absolute rules. The best choice depends on the cultivar and the recipe.

Color and appearance

Cultivated potatoes may have white, cream, yellow, gold, red, purple, or blue skin and flesh. Yellow and gold colors can be associated with carotenoids. Red and blue pigmentation is largely associated with anthocyanins and other polyphenols. Color can be useful for identifying varieties, but it does not by itself determine the potato's cooking behavior or overall nutritional value.

Examples of varieties or market types

  • Yukon Gold: A yellow-fleshed type commonly sold in North America.
  • Red potatoes: Often sold as red-skinned, cream-fleshed potatoes.
  • Jersey Royal: A well-known new-potato type associated with Jersey in the United Kingdom.
  • Andean landraces: A broad range of traditional varieties grown in the Andes, often with distinctive colors and adaptations to local conditions.

For a website, a variety guide could list each cultivar's skin and flesh color, texture, best uses, growing season, storage qualities, and regional availability.

6. Growing potatoes

Planting material

Potatoes are commonly grown from seed potatoes: tubers selected to produce healthy, consistent plants. Certified or carefully selected seed potatoes reduce the risk of carrying diseases into a crop. Potatoes can also be grown from botanical seeds in breeding programs, but tuber planting is the usual method for crop production.

Growth stages

Potato development can be divided into five broad phases:

  1. Sprouting and rooting: Shoots emerge from the seed tuber and roots begin to grow.
  2. Vegetative growth: Leaves and branches develop, photosynthesis increases, and underground stolons form.
  3. Tuber initiation: Stolons begin to swell at their tips, creating young tubers; flowering may occur around this period.
  4. Tuber bulking: The plant directs much of its energy into increasing tuber size. Soil moisture, temperature, nutrients, and pest pressure strongly influence yield.
  5. Maturation: Leaves and stems age, tuber growth slows, and skins become firmer.

Soil and climate

Potatoes generally perform best in temperate conditions and well-drained soil. Commercial production commonly uses neutral to mildly acidic soils; the article gives a typical pH range around 6–7. Soil structure, moisture, nutrient balance, sunlight, and temperature affect yield. Potatoes are vulnerable to frost, while excessive heat can reduce growth and tuber quality. Temperatures above about 30 °C (86 °F) can be harmful.

Planting and care

Commercial crops are planted in rows using machinery. Home gardeners can plant tubers in trenches, holes, containers, or growing bags, using free-draining soil or compost. Gardeners commonly cover the base of plants with additional soil or mulch as the shoots grow.

Hilling or earthing up covers developing tubers and prevents light exposure, which can cause greening and increased glycoalkaloid levels. Adequate and even soil moisture is important, especially while tubers are bulking, but waterlogged soil is undesirable.

Pests and diseases

Important potato pests and diseases include:

  • Late blight: Caused by Phytophthora infestans; historically responsible for devastating crop losses.
  • Rhizoctonia and powdery scab: Diseases that can affect plant growth or tuber quality.
  • Blackleg and related bacterial diseases: Can damage stems and plants.
  • Leafroll virus and other viruses: May reduce plant vigor and yield.
  • Colorado potato beetle: A major leaf-feeding insect capable of damaging entire crops.
  • Potato tuber moth: Can damage plants and tubers.
  • Aphids: Damage plants and transmit viruses.
  • Potato cyst nematodes: Microscopic pests that attack roots and may persist in soil for years.

Crop rotation, healthy planting material, monitoring, resistant cultivars, sanitation, and locally appropriate integrated pest management can reduce risks. Specific treatment recommendations should be based on local agricultural guidance.

Harvesting and curing

Small crops can be harvested by hand or with simple tools. Commercial farms use potato harvesters that lift plants and soil, separate tubers from debris, and transport the crop for sorting. Potatoes are often cured after harvest under controlled warm, humid conditions to help skins set and wounds heal. This reduces moisture loss and the chance of infection during storage.

Storage

Potatoes should be stored in a dark, well-ventilated place. Light exposure can cause greening and increase glycoalkaloids. Commercial long-term storage uses carefully controlled temperature, humidity, and ventilation to slow sprouting and prevent condensation. The article describes storage temperatures near 4 °C for some long-term commercial systems and higher temperatures for shorter-term storage; household advice may differ depending on local food-safety guidance and intended use.

Very cold storage can convert some starch into sugars, changing flavor and cooking behavior and potentially increasing acrylamide formation during high-temperature cooking. Storage conditions should balance sprout control, quality, and intended processing use.

7. Global production and food security

Potatoes are a major food crop grown across temperate and many other suitable regions. Production has historically been important in Europe, while production growth in recent decades has been especially strong in Asia.

Potatoes are important because they provide substantial food energy, can be grown in varied environments, and are consumed in many cultural cuisines. Their contribution to food security depends on seed quality, storage, transport, access to water, market conditions, and the ability to manage pests and diseases.

8. Climate change and the future of potato production

Climate change can affect potato yields through rising temperatures, changing rainfall, water stress, altered growing seasons, and changes in the distribution of pests and diseases.

  • Potatoes generally grow best in temperate conditions.
  • Heat can slow growth, reduce starch accumulation, damage tubers, and lower yields.
  • Water shortages can be especially damaging during tuber formation and bulking.
  • Frost and low temperatures can also limit growth and damage crops.
  • Warmer conditions may allow some pests and pathogens to spread into new areas.
  • The effects of late blight may increase in some regions and decrease in others, depending on local temperature and moisture patterns.

Potential adaptation strategies include breeding for heat and drought tolerance, early maturation, disease resistance, improved water-use efficiency, and deeper root systems. Irrigation, improved agronomy, and shifting production toward cooler locations may also help, though they can create competition for land and water. Projections vary by region and depend on future climate, crop varieties, and adaptation.

9. Nutrition

Potatoes are primarily a source of carbohydrates, especially starch. Their nutritional profile changes with variety, cooking method, whether the skin is eaten, and added ingredients such as oil, butter, salt, or sauces.

Nutritional context

  • Potatoes provide energy mainly through carbohydrates.
  • They contain very little fat when boiled without added ingredients.
  • They provide vitamin C, vitamin B6, some other B vitamins, and potassium.
  • Eating the skin contributes dietary fiber.
  • Cooking method matters: deep-frying and high-fat toppings can substantially increase energy and fat content.
  • The glycemic index varies by cultivar, preparation, and cooking method. Some potato preparations can cause a relatively rapid rise in blood glucose.
  • Raw potato starch is poorly digested, so potatoes are generally eaten cooked.

The Wikipedia article notes that evidence on the relationship between potato consumption and obesity or diabetes is not definitive. A website should avoid making medical claims and should distinguish the plain potato from the nutritional profile of a prepared dish.

10. Toxicity and safe handling

Potatoes contain natural glycoalkaloids, especially solanine and chaconine, which help protect the plant from pests. These compounds are concentrated in the plant's above-ground parts and are normally present at low levels in properly grown and stored edible tubers.

Light exposure, greening, physical damage, and age can increase glycoalkaloid levels. Sprouts and green areas may contain higher concentrations. Cooking does not reliably make a heavily green or toxic potato safe.

Practical safety guidance:

  • Store potatoes in a cool, dark, well-ventilated location.
  • Avoid eating potatoes that are substantially green, badly damaged, or heavily sprouted.
  • Remove small sprouts and affected areas only when the potato otherwise appears sound; discard potatoes with extensive greening or deterioration.
  • Do not eat the plant's green fruits or other above-ground parts.
  • When uncertain about a potato's condition, discard it rather than risk poisoning.

For a public-facing website, safety advice should be reviewed against current food-safety guidance from relevant public-health authorities.

11. Culinary uses around the world

Potatoes are prepared in many ways: boiled, steamed, baked, roasted, mashed, fried, grated, dried, and processed into flour or starch. Their texture and starch content influence which preparation works best.

Familiar preparations

  • French fries or chips
  • Potato crisps
  • Baked potatoes
  • Mashed potatoes
  • Roasted potatoes
  • Boiled new potatoes
  • Potato salad
  • Soups and stews
  • Potato pancakes and fritters
  • Dumplings and pasta-like preparations

Regional examples

  • Peru and Bolivia: Potatoes are central to Andean food traditions. Chuño is a traditional freeze-dried potato product associated with Quechua and Aymara communities.
  • United Kingdom: Fish and chips, roast potatoes, mashed potatoes, and other traditional dishes feature potatoes.
  • Germany and Northern and Eastern Europe: Boiled new potatoes, often served with dill and fish, are traditional in various regions.
  • Lithuania: Cepelinai are potato-based dumplings, often filled with meat.
  • Italy: Potatoes are used to make gnocchi in some regional cuisines.
  • China: Thinly sliced potato stir-fries and roasted potatoes are among the many local preparations.
  • Jewish, Lithuanian, and Belarusian cuisines: Grated-potato puddings and baked dishes include kugel, kugelis, and potato babka.

A recipe section on a potato website could organize dishes by cooking method, region, potato texture, preparation time, and dietary needs.

12. Uses beyond everyday cooking

Potatoes and their components have several non-table-food uses:

  • Potato starch: Used as a thickener or binder in foods such as soups and sauces.
  • Industrial starch: Used in paper and board manufacturing and as an adhesive in textile applications.
  • Distilled spirits: Potatoes have been used as a raw material for spirits such as vodka, poitín, akvavit, and brännvin.
  • Animal feed: Potatoes may be fed to livestock or preserved as silage.
  • Scientific research: Potato tissue is used in plant research, including studies of wound responses and cellular processes.

13. Cultural significance and representation

The potato is more than an agricultural commodity; it is connected to Indigenous traditions, regional identity, art, popular culture, and stories about food security.

Mythology and Indigenous traditions

In Inca mythology, Axomamma is a goddess associated with potatoes and agricultural fertility. The Wikipedia article also describes a Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) story in which potatoes grow from Earth Woman after her death. Such stories should be presented with cultural context and reliable references rather than treated as interchangeable traditions.

Art

Potatoes and potato harvesting appear in works depicting rural life and labor. Vincent van Gogh's The Potato Eaters (1885) portrays a family sharing a meal. Jean-François Millet's The Potato Harvest and other European paintings show the crop's relationship to peasant work and survival. Potato imagery also appears in pre-Columbian Andean art, including Moche ceramics.

Popular culture

  • Mr. Potato Head: A toy introduced commercially in the early 1950s, consisting of a potato-shaped body and attachable facial features and accessories.
  • The Martian (2015): The fictional astronaut and botanist Mark Watney grows potatoes while stranded on Mars, using the crop as a symbol of practical survival and food production under extreme conditions.

Before publishing this material:

  • Verify global production figures against the latest FAOSTAT data.
